# Clay vs PostHog: Sales Motion Comparison

This guide compares the sales motions of Clay and PostHog - two companies that have rejected traditional sales tactics in favor of more authentic, technical, and customer-centric approaches.

## Executive Summary

Both Clay and PostHog share a fundamental belief: **traditional sales tactics don't work anymore**. They've both built non-salesy motions that prioritize technical competency, authenticity, and solving real problems over quota-hitting pressure tactics.

However, their specific implementations differ significantly based on their products, ICPs, and go-to-market stages.

---

## Company Overviews

### Clay
- **Product**: Data enrichment and GTM automation platform
- **ICP**: Sales/marketing teams, growth leads, founders
- **GTM Stage**: Scaled from $0 to $30M+ ARR in ~2 years, now $1B+ valuation
- **Model**: PLG foundation with layered sales-led motion

### PostHog
- **Product**: All-in-one product analytics platform
- **ICP**: Product engineers at high-growth startups (Series B to IPO)
- **GTM Stage**: Primarily inbound, experimenting with outbound
- **Model**: Strong PLG, sales for $20k+ customers

---

## Core Similarities

### 1. Rejection of Traditional Sales Tactics

**Clay:**
> "All of this is a culture of experimentation, iteration, and not copying other companies' playbooks, which is my least favorite thing that companies do."

**PostHog:**
> "We don't want to end up taking cold leads from BDRs so we can run MEDPICC from our car phone while promising 50% discounts if they sign before the next full moon."

Both companies explicitly warn against:
- High-pressure closing
- Generic cold outreach
- Complex sales methodologies executed poorly
- Copying what other companies do

---

### 2. Technical Sales People (Not Traditional Salespeople)

**Clay - GTM Engineers:**
- Former founders, engineers, growth experts
- Head of Sales is an engineer by training with no sales experience
- "High-agency folks with a technical bent"
- Can solve any use case "at the drop of a hat"

**PostHog - Technical BDRs:**
- Must "carry a sales conversation while possessing technical chops to talk to engineers"
- "Own technical problems" - not pass them to engineering
- Be the primary person solving customer problems

**Shared Philosophy:** Both hire for technical aptitude over sales experience. They want people who can actually solve problems, not just book meetings.

---

### 3. Self-Serve PLG Foundation

**Clay:**
- Self-serve is primary revenue motion
- "At our price point, self-serve had to work"
- 2-week reverse trial with Pro features
- Sales layered on top for larger deals

**PostHog:**
- Generous free tier (1M events, 5K recordings/month)
- Self-serve everything, including Enterprise ($2k/month)
- No "talk to sales" wall
- Sales focuses on $20k+ potential customers

**Shared Philosophy:** Let the product sell itself first. Sales adds value for larger, more complex deals - not for qualification or gatekeeping.

---

### 4. Transparency and Honesty

**Clay:**
- "Clay is very transparent. We tell you exactly which individual providers your data comes from"
- Published detailed memos explaining pricing changes
- Public Slack community exposes product shortcomings
- Building in public with blog posts about their journey

**PostHog:**
- Everything public: code, handbook, roadmap, investor info, compensation
- "Be honest when you don't know, or when PostHog isn't the right solution"
- No BS sales talk - direct, open, honest
- Share as much as possible publicly

**Shared Philosophy:** Transparency builds trust, especially with technical audiences. Hiding information destroys credibility.

---

### 5. Solving Real Problems Over Selling

**Clay - Reverse Demo:**
- Customer brings real dataset and real problem
- Solve their actual problem in 30 minutes
- "Earn trust by solving real problems in ways that surprise and delight"

**PostHog - No Unnecessary Friction:**
- "Most companies add friction by making customers jump on a call first to qualify them. PostHog doesn't do this."
- Focus on making paying customers successful
- Qualify out bad fits rather than forcing them through

**Shared Philosophy:** Focus on delivering value, not extracting commitments. If you solve real problems, revenue follows.

---

### 6. Community as Growth Engine

**Clay:**
- 15,000-20,000 member Slack community
- Started as cost-saving (shut down Intercom), became growth engine
- Peer-to-peer learning and support
- "Claygencies" ecosystem emerged

**PostHog:**
- GitHub-centric async community
- Open source community contributions
- Engineers recommend tools to each other
- Word-of-mouth from ICP to ICP

**Shared Philosophy:** Your best customers become your best salespeople when you deliver exceptional value.

---

### 7. Culture of Experimentation

**Clay:**
- "Culture of experimentation, iteration"
- Revised pricing twice with public explanations
- Tried unconventional things (public Slack, waitlist for 15 months)

**PostHog:**
- Running outbound experiments (hence the Founding BDR role)
- "Why not now?" - bias for action
- Learn by doing, not hypothesizing

**Shared Philosophy:** Don't assume what works elsewhere will work for you. Test, learn, iterate.

## Key Differences

### 1. Sales Interaction Model

**Clay - Reverse Demo (High Touch):**
- Customer shares screen
- Clay rep guides them through solving their problem live
- 30-minute sessions, 8 per day at peak
- Zoom annotation to guide clicks
- "Our goal was to blow their minds"

**PostHog - Minimal Friction (Low Touch):**
- Let qualified prospects self-serve
- Don't force unnecessary calls
- Only add sales where it truly adds value
- Focus on $20k+ where sales effort is justified

**Key Insight:** Clay's product is complex and benefits from guided onboarding. PostHog's autocapture means customers can get value immediately without help. The reverse demo wouldn't necessarily work for PostHog's product, but the *principle* of solving real problems applies.

---

### 2. Community Platform

**Clay - Public Slack:**
- Real-time support
- All customers in same channels
- Fast feedback loop
- Public visibility of problems

**PostHog - GitHub/Async:**
- Issues and PRs are public
- Documentation-first
- Async by default
- Written communication emphasized

**Key Insight:** Both use community, but in ways suited to their audiences. Clay's users are sales/marketing people who live in Slack. PostHog's users are engineers who live in GitHub.

---

### 3. Role Definition

**Clay - GTM Engineer:**
- Distinct new role they invented
- Blend of sales, marketing, and engineering
- "Half commercial thinker, half builder"
- Creates content, runs demos, relays feedback

**PostHog - Technical BDR:**
- More traditional title, non-traditional execution
- First outbound role (they're building the motion)
- Focus on qualification and prospecting
- Must be technical enough to own problems

**Key Insight:** Clay has scaled further and created a new job category. PostHog is earlier in building their outbound motion, so the role is more exploratory.

---

### 4. Hiring Philosophy Nuance

**Clay:**
- "Over half their non-engineering team is in roles they've never done"
- Deliberately hires people without job experience
- Prioritizes unique perspectives over proven playbooks

**PostHog:**
- "Culture fit > skills" (skills can be taught)
- Many team members are former founders
- Emphasizes self-starters who don't need management

**Key Insight:** Both prioritize non-traditional candidates, but Clay is more explicit about hiring people who've never done the job. PostHog focuses more on founder mentality and autonomy.

---

### 5. GTM Stage

**Clay:**
- Scaled to $30M+ ARR
- Has sales-led motion for enterprise
- GTM Engineer team of ~14 people
- Proven playbook they're now teaching

**PostHog:**
- Primarily inbound still
- Experimenting with outbound
- Founding BDR = first of this role
- Building the playbook from scratch

**Key Insight:** As Founding BDR at PostHog, you're more like early Clay (building from scratch) than current Clay (scaling proven motion).

## Lessons from Clay for PostHog BDR Role

### 1. The Reverse Demo Principle

You probably won't do literal reverse demos at PostHog, but the principle applies:

**Instead of:** "Let me show you our features"
**Do:** "What problem are you trying to solve? Let's solve it together."

This means:
- Come to calls prepared to help, not pitch
- Know the product deeply enough to solve any use case
- Let the customer drive the conversation
- Surprise and delight them with real solutions

---

### 2. GTM Engineer Mindset

Even if your title is "BDR," think like Clay's GTM Engineers:

- **Be technical**: Solve problems yourself, don't pass to engineering
- **Be creative**: Find novel ways to help each prospect
- **Be a builder**: Create systems and processes, not just activity
- **Be nimble**: Handle any use case at the drop of a hat

---

### 3. Community Involvement

Clay's Slack became a growth engine. For PostHog:

- Engage where engineers are (GitHub, Hacker News, technical communities)
- Share genuine learnings, not sales content
- Help people even if they're not prospects
- Build reputation as someone who solves problems

---

### 4. Experimentation Mindset

Both companies emphasize experimentation:

- You're building PostHog's outbound playbook
- Test different approaches
- Document what works
- Don't assume traditional BDR tactics will work
- Be willing to throw away what doesn't work

---

### 5. Technical Credibility

Both companies hire technical salespeople because:

- Their ICPs are technical (engineers, growth leads)
- Generic sales talk destroys credibility instantly
- Solving real problems requires understanding them
- Trust comes from competence, not charm

## Tactical Applications

### Outbound Messaging

**Apply Clay's Principle:**
Don't pitch. Offer to solve a specific problem.

**Bad:** "I'd love to show you how PostHog can help your team"
**Better:** "I noticed you're using [competitor] for analytics and [other tool] for feature flags. We consolidated those for [similar company] - would it be useful if I showed you how?"

---

### Discovery Calls

**Apply Reverse Demo Principle:**
Let them show you their problem. Solve it live.

**Instead of:** Running through a deck
**Do:** "Can you share your screen? Let's look at what you're trying to understand about your users and set it up together."

---

### Qualification

**Apply Both Companies' Honesty:**
Qualify out bad fits quickly.

**Clay:** Only did reverse demos for ICP
**PostHog:** "Be honest when PostHog isn't the right solution"

If someone isn't a fit, tell them. Recommend alternatives. They'll remember you when they are a fit later.

---

### Follow-ups

**Apply Value-First Principle:**
Every touch should provide value.

**Bad:** "Just checking in on this"
**Better:** "Here's a guide we just published on [their specific problem]. Thought it might help regardless of whether we work together."

## Summary: Combined Playbook

Based on both Clay and PostHog's approaches, here's the synthesized playbook:

### Core Beliefs
1. Traditional sales tactics don't work for technical buyers
2. Solving real problems beats selling features
3. Trust comes from competence and honesty
4. Community amplifies word-of-mouth
5. Self-serve PLG is the foundation; sales adds value on top

### Behaviors
1. Be technical enough to solve problems yourself
2. Be honest about fit (including when you're not the right solution)
3. Focus on customer success, not just closing
4. Experiment and iterate; don't copy playbooks
5. Provide value in every interaction

### Anti-Patterns
1. High-pressure tactics
2. Generic outreach
3. Feature dumping
4. Forcing unnecessary meetings
5. Passing technical problems to engineering
